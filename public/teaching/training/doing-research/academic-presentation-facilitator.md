# Delivering an Academic Presentation

Doing Research · Session 3 · April Jiawei Zhang · City University of Macau

## Purpose and preparation

A 90-minute workshop for master's and doctoral researchers preparing a conference talk or research seminar. Participants develop one defensible contribution, explain evidence, rehearse delivery and practise answering questions. The lesson opens in English; the top-right button translates the current slide into Traditional Chinese. Each slide retains its chosen language during navigation.

Ask participants to bring an abstract, a draft slide or one finding. Researchers without results can present their proposed design, clearly distinguishing planned work from completed work. Arrange pairs for brief practice and triads for the final rehearsal. Paper responses work equally well if participants do not have devices.

## Facilitation sequence

| --- | --- | --- |

| 08–23 | 4–8: audience, message, structure, timing and opening | Give four minutes for the central-message task. Partners repeat the message before advising. Use the timing calculator to distinguish speaking time from the total slot. Participants write and read an opening. |
| 23–43 | 9–15: methods, slide design and evidence | Explain the minimum methodological information needed to judge a claim. Allow five minutes for slide repair and five for the 60-second evidence exercise. Use the qualitative or quantitative example most relevant to the group; treat the other as a short comparison. Discuss mixed-methods integration where relevant. |
| 43–55 | 16–19, then 24: delivery, rehearsal and closing | Model the same sentence with and without meaningful pauses. Invite a participant to explain the difference. Practise one transition. Jump to slide 24 through the overview to prepare a concise closing before the micro-talk. |
| 55–65 | 20–21: Q&A | Model a direct, qualified answer. Give pairs different difficult questions. Hear an answer before opening the suggested response. Ask what evidence would be needed to make a stronger claim. |
| 65–85 | 22–23: triad rehearsal | Each round: three-minute talk, one-minute Q&A, one-minute feedback. Rotate roles three times, then use five minutes for revision and a second attempt at an opening or closing. Show the feedback criteria before starting. |

## Teaching notes

- All numerical examples and quoted learner speech in this lesson are invented for teaching. Do not present them as empirical findings or attribute them to a real publication.
- In the slide-repair exercise, the original paragraph cannot establish the proposed replacement headline. Participants should notice the missing evidence and check actual results before writing a claim.
- The quantitative figure represents an estimated 2.1-point difference on a 0–20 revision scale, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.6 to 3.6. Discuss magnitude, uncertainty and the limits of non-random assignment. Do not interpret the interval as a probability that this fixed parameter lies inside it.
- In the qualitative example, ask how the interpretation would be supported across a dataset and what contrary cases might change it. A compelling quotation is not sufficient evidence of prevalence.
- In a mixed-methods presentation, ask what is learned through integration. Confirm whether the two strands concern the same participants and time points before linking them.
- Evaluate communicative choices, not accent, personality, disability or conformity to a particular speaking style. Adapt audience orientation and notes to the presenter’s access needs.
- Rehearsal recordings are optional. Use them only with participants' agreement and appropriate arrangements; the lesson does not record audio or video.

## Feedback protocol

For each criterion, use **clear / developing / not yet observable**, supported by an example:

1. Message and audience: the contribution can be accurately restated.
2. Argument and evidence: claims answer the question and match the design.
3. Visual explanation: the audience understands the evidence and its importance.
4. Delivery and time: the talk is audible, connected and completed within the limit.
5. Response to questions: answers are direct, supported and appropriately qualified.

Use the stems: “The message I heard was…”; “At this moment, your explanation helped because…”; “One change to try is…”. Ask the presenter to explain which suggestion they will adopt and why.

## Adaptations and participant outputs

For 60 minutes, use one evidence example, shorten whole-group reporting and retain the full triad rehearsal. For 120 minutes, add a second rehearsal after revision and compare the two performances using the same criteria.

Participants leave with a central message, an audience statement, a six-part argument, an opening, an evidence explanation, a transition, a closing, one anticipated question, peer feedback and a rehearsal action. The page keeps responses in the current browser where storage is available; the text download provides a separate copy. Responses are not sent to the teacher. Printing uses the current slide's language throughout and includes entered responses.
