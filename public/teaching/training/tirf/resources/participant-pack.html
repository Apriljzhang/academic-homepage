Part 1: understand formative assessment

Informing versus forming: information about performance informs. Formative use requires evidence-informed action intended to close an identified gap, with learner take-up and a way to check the effect. Do not infer development from feedback being displayed.

Weekly reflective blogs: informing only, non-formative as presented

Students describe what was taught and what puzzles them. The teacher tracks understanding, language use and engagement. The original example does not show purposeful developmental follow-up. Our proposed redesign: investigate a recurring difficulty and its cause, provide focused support, ask learners to use a strategy in a new task, then inspect fresh evidence. This is a plan, not observed development.

Dictation: a conditional example

1. Give dictation. 2. Learners mark work/highlight correct forms. 3. Teacher/learner receive information about spelling, listening and grammar. 4. Information is used to modify teaching or guide self-study. Steps 1–3 inform; step 4 is formative only when the enacted action is intended to close the identified gap and its effect is checked. A vague promise to “modify instruction” is insufficient.

Then ask: what are these learners trying to get better at?

A supplied textbook example lists reading about the virtual choir, discussing music preferences, writing a speech about how music has helped someone, and learning about film scores. These provide content and language activities, but do not specify the language use that these learners need to develop. If evidence shows learners state preferences but cannot explain them, a conditional workshop aim is to explain a preference with a relevant reason and concrete example. This rewrite is not attributed to the unidentified textbook.

A dictation error could reflect perception, spelling, grammar, vocabulary or attention. Check the interpretation before choosing support. Textbook content is a resource; learner needs inform the actionable language focus.

How the five strategies appear in our language activity
  1. Clarifying, sharing and understanding learning intentions. Make the language focus and two success criteria visible and understood.
  2. Engineering effective discussions, tasks, and activities that elicit evidence of learning. Ask learners to choose, justify and write so the reasoning becomes visible.
  3. Providing feedback that moves learners forward. Offer a checked hint that enables a purposeful next step.
  4. Activating students as learning resources for one another. Test a partner’s activity and discuss what the responses show.
  5. Activating students as owners of their own learning. Revise, explain the change and inspect fresh evidence.

Targets at the centre of formative practice

In the supplied framework attributed to Li and Gu (2024), formative purpose connects to formative practice, then to possible formative effect. Targets of learning connect both ways with each practice: eliciting evidence → interpreting evidence → formative feedback → learner/teacher take-up and action → eliciting evidence. Targets are revisited throughout, not completed once. The supplied diagram is redrawn interactively in the workshop; targets remain central to every practice.

Distinguish these five elements from Black and Wiliam’s five strategies: clarify goals/criteria; elicit evidence; feedback that moves learning forward; peers as resources; learners as owners.

Stop, investigate or support

Goal met → move on/extend or confirm the planned next step. Evidence unclear → diagnostic follow-up. Gap evident → focused support and a useful fresh recheck, which can occur in a later task. Do not force endless cycles.

Evidence and boundaries

Effects depend on implementation and use of evidence; more quizzes, feedback or AI do not guarantee improvement. No promise to double learning. Published estimates vary by context and implementation; they do not forecast this activity’s effect. A sound process still needs its effects checked, while no observed gain does not automatically make the process invalid.

Part 2: use GenAI for formative purposes

GenAI creates text, images, audio and other content in response to prompts; coding tools can build an activity. Teachers and learners remain responsible for purposeful use of evidence. Ordinary digital quizzes, clickers and portfolios are not necessarily AI; neither category guarantees formative use.

Targets and criteria

The tool may help: Suggest a knowledge/performance focus, draft criteria or synthesise curriculum and textbook material.
We remain responsible: Use learner evidence, needs and professional judgement to choose the language focus. Textbook synthesis is a resource, not the aim itself.

Eliciting evidence

The tool may help: Generate and deliver a task; structure responses; mark suitable closed items where this is actually implemented.
We remain responsible: Check that the task elicits the intended language ability and that instructions do not create misleading errors.

Interpreting evidence

The tool may help: Organise evidence or suggest patterns and possible gaps.
We remain responsible: Validate the inference against the criteria and consider alternatives. A pattern is not an unquestioned diagnosis.

Feedback

The tool may help: Draft strengths, weaknesses, hints or suggested activities.
We remain responsible: Verify accuracy and fit. Information about performance informs; suggestions become formative use only through purposeful take-up and action.

Take-up and action

The tool may help: Build a scaffold, practice tool or tutor where the required capability is genuinely implemented.
We remain responsible: Teachers and learners enact action intended to close the gap and check fresh evidence. Our offline lab uses fixed local hints, not an intelligent tutor or runtime AI.

Peer-test checklist

  • □ Strong response: both criteria visible; reflection and next action possible.
  • □ Weak response: a relevant hint, no invented grade.
  • □ Blank and whitespace: hints wait for an attempt.
  • □ Long/unusual text: exact writing is preserved and the layout remains usable.
  • □ Keyboard and mobile: all controls labelled, reachable and readable.
  • □ Reset: clear confirmation. Print/export: actual before-and-after text.
  • □ No network, persistence, runtime AI or cross-device response collection.

Fictional evidence → action cases

These are provisional hypotheses, not ability labels. Consider alternative explanations. The teacher retains the decision.

L01: Selects B because it is longer; adds an unrelated anecdote.

Possible interpretation: May be using length as a proxy for support. The task wording may also have directed attention to length.
Action: Investigate relevance using equally long contrasting examples.
Recheck: Ask for a fresh choice and a justification that explains relevance.

L02: Identifies the example but omits its explanation.

Possible interpretation: May recognise an example but need support explaining its link; time pressure is another possibility.
Action: Model a because-link between one example and the claim.
Recheck: Ask the learner to explain a new example without the model.

L03: Chooses A because they agree and repeats the claim.

Possible interpretation: May be treating agreement as support; the distinction may not yet be clear from the instructions.
Action: Contrast agreeing with a claim and giving evidence for it.
Recheck: Request a new specific example and an explanation of how it supports the claim.

L04: Provides a specific relevant example and explains its link.

Possible interpretation: Both criteria are evident in this response. A familiar topic could have helped.
Action: Offer transfer to a fresh topic or invite the learner to explain the criteria to a peer.
Recheck: Inspect the example and link on a fresh topic or in the peer explanation.
